I Screened YC's Fall 2026 Batch: 142 In, 5 Out

Which companies could raise a Series A within 18 months?

By Jin Zhi · October 18, 2026

In startup circles, "YC-backed" is still a gold badge, but YC's batches are inflating faster than tips in New York. YC now runs four batches a year, and 2026 is on track for roughly 770 companies, including 142 in Fall so far.

At this rate, just keeping up with YC companies is heavy work. So I decided to play a diligent investor and dig into all 142 companies in the Fall 2026 batch, using nothing but public information.

The goal

Let's set the goal: find the companies most likely to raise a priced Series A within 18 months.

First, think about this: what type of investor would set a goal like this?

In my observation, two kinds:

  1. Emerging managers, who need markups to raise their next fund. A priced Series A is the clearest early one.
  2. Platforms that give individual investors early access. Their investors want visible progress and, eventually, a way out, and a priced round gives them a milestone and a path to liquidity.

In the end, it comes down to whether the Series A investors say yes. They weigh a lot of things before the "yes", but two are hard to get around, one about now and one about what comes next:

  1. Now: the demand has to be real. It starts with a buyer who already pays for what the product replaces, so the decision to buy is easier and faster. And it has to be verifiable: paying customers and live deployments are hard to fake; adjectives aren't.
  2. Next: the company has to keep winning as things change. Both the product and the market will keep changing. The product will keep evolving as customers use it, and founders who know the problem firsthand, or bring a rare skill, are better placed to steer it. The market will shift too, as competitors and model labs move in, and only a lead that gets harder to copy over time survives that.

How I screened

These concerns became my four tests. The first two rest on facts and the last two on judgment, so I ran them in that order and counted only public evidence. A company was out at the first test it failed.

1. Replaces existing spendThe product replaces a specific cost already in the buyer's budget, such as a role, an outside service or a tool.97
2. Verifiable demandSomeone outside the team has paid for the product or put it into live operations: named paying customers or a deployment with numbers. Logo walls, free trials, waitlists, GitHub stars and investor backing don't count.14
3. Founder–market fitA founder has worked inside the problem or brings a scarce technical skill. Prestigious credentials with no link to the problem don't count.10
4. MoatThe company gets harder to replace as it grows, through proprietary data, regulated workflows or network effects. Speed or a better interface alone doesn't count.5

What's not on the list. Market size. A Series A investor will ask about it, but at batch stage a market estimate is hard to verify from outside, and under an 18-month clock, proof beats potential. A fund hunting for outliers would put it first.

A note on hardware. Hardware companies usually need more capital and a longer product and customer validation cycle to get from seed to Series A, so they sit less naturally with an 18-month path to a priced round. That shows in the screen: 29 companies, about a fifth of the batch, build hardware or physical systems, and none made it past the demand test. The one I would watch first is Rodeo Robotics, which builds robots for vehicle maintenance and says it earns service revenue while collecting robot training data, though it hasn't disclosed customers or volume.

What I found

In the end, only 5 of the 142 companies passed all four tests.

But the bigger cut came earlier. Of the 97 companies that cleared the first test, only 14 got through the second. Most could describe what they're building, but couldn't name a customer.

These five show promising signals, and a few question marks. The Fall 2026 batch is still ongoing, with Demo Day on December 2. As a more cautious "investor," I'll keep watching and take a closer look at a few of them along the way.
